# T3 Brief — Behavioral evidence: why expense tracking fails

Research evidence that manual expense tracking fails for real users, and what reduces friction enough to work.

Find:

1. **Attrition/friction evidence**: studies, surveys, or articles on why people abandon expense trackers/budgets (forgetting to log, setup cost, guilt/shame, judgment fatigue). Look for habit-formation or quantified-self research on logging frequency.
2. **Friction-reducing capture modes that work**: voice/photo/chat capture vs manual forms; any data on "capture in ≤5 seconds" claim.
3. India-specific context: UPI transaction explosion (NPCI data), cash+tiffin economy, monthly budgeting culture, why aggregator apps break for cash/tiffin-style spending.
4. **Design philosophies**: YNAB's philosophy, Copilot/Monarch UX positioning — do any promise "awareness" rather than "restriction"? Which succeed, and does the research support non-judgmental framing (constraints) over hard restriction?

## Deliverable

Write findings to `outputs/.drafts/pfos-research-behavior.md`: the problem is real + evidenced, paragraph-level citations with URLs, and 3–5 short bullets on what makes capture survive beyond 3 weeks of usage. This brief feeds the "Impact" section of the pitch deck later.

Rules:
- Use websearch + webfetch. Prefer survey/research pieces and reputable finance/tech journalism.
- Record exact URL next to each claim. No fabricated stats — if a number can't be source-pinned, mark it "unverified" or drop it.
- Aim ≤ 8 distinct searches.
- Note disagreements or conflicting findings if you encounter them.
