# T3 — Behavioral Evidence: Why Expense Tracking Fails, and What Makes It Stick

Research brief for the PFOS pitch deck "Impact" axis. Compiled 2026-11-03. Every claim carries its source URL; anything not source-pinned or that comes only from an app vendor's own blog is explicitly marked.

---

## 1. The problem is real and evidenced: manual expense tracking is abandoned within weeks

**Abandonment is the default outcome, and logging friction is the primary cause.** A peer-reviewed HCI study (Epstein et al., CHI 2016, UW/Facebook/Microsoft) surveyed 193 people who had formerly tracked physical activity, finances, or location and interviewed 12. The single most reported category of reasons for quitting was the **"cost of collecting and integrating" data** — the effort of remembering and entering what happened — and a distinct finance-specific pain emerged: **discomfort with what the data revealed**. One participant "felt guilty every time I tracked an expense that was not a necessity"; another "didn't like being so aware of how little money I had." Prior food-journaling work had already shown logs can feel judgmental when people exceed budgets; the authors extend this to financial tracking and conclude that "abandoning tracking may be the easiest way to address this discomfort." (https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jfogarty/publications/chi2016-abandonment.pdf, also https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5428074)

**Forgetting to log is documented in a government study.** A U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics evaluation of expense-tracking apps for the Consumer Expenditure Survey (96 apps scanned, feasibility study with real participants) found the dominant complaint was that **it was difficult to remember to enter expenses** — participants wrote "It is easily forgettable," "Forgot to log some expenses. Forgot to constantly update the info," and asked for built-in pop-up reminders. Notably, the same study found participants **praised voice recognition** ("Liked the voice recognition feature, it was much easier than typing on that small keyboard") and wanted receipt-photo capture to work better — early evidence that voice/photo capture reduces the very friction that kills tracking. (https://www.bls.gov/cex/research_papers/pdf/evaluation-of-financial-application-software-report.pdf)

**The quantitative retention cliff (with a caveat on sourcing).** Industry retention data consistently shows finance apps bleed out early: the Money Vault aggregator piece reports finance-app day-30 retention at ~8.7% and day-90 at ~3.4%, with 74% of downloads never opened a second time (https://aimoneyvault.app/resources/articles/expense-tracking-statistics-2026). **Caveat: this is a vendor-adjacent aggregation blog; treat as unverified provenance.** What is independently solid is RevenueCat's State of Subscription Apps data across categories: **1.7% of downloads became paying subscribers in the first 30 days** (2024 report, https://www.revenuecat.com/pdf/state-of-subscription-apps-2024.pdf), and in the 2026 report **AI-powered apps generate 41% more revenue per customer but churn 30% faster** on 12-month retention — a direct warning for an AI-finance product (https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps). The widely cited "85% abandon within weeks" figure appears only in vendor blogs (e.g., https://www.gaussapp.com/en/blog/why-financial-apps-fail.html) — **unverified; do not use as a hard stat**.

**Self-tracking research generalizes the pattern.** Attig & Franke (Computers in Human Behavior, 2020) show "many users discontinue tracking after only a few months," and cite research that **one-third of consumers stop using activity trackers within 6 months**; permanent abandonment was most linked to loss of tracking motivation (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219303127). Epstein et al.'s follow-up "lived informatics" model formalizes a **"lapsing" stage** — users taper off use rather than deciding to quit, which matches the observed pattern of "miss one expense, guilt spiral, silent abandonment" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12435389). Habit research underpins the timeline: habits "do not develop immediately (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts, & Wardle, 2010)" — i.e., most manual loggers quit before the behavior ever became automatic (cited in https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563219303127).

**India-specific dimension: the tracker stack has collapsed, and cash spending was never captured anyway.** Walnut — India's dominant SMS-scraping expense tracker — was acquired and rebranded into Axio, pivoting to credit/lending, so "the pure, no-nonsense expense tracker faded into the background"; the tracker "worked because you never had to type anything in," which is now effectively gone (https://spendctrl.com/guides/axio-walnut-alternative, corroborated by https://fintrakk.com/best-personal-expense-tracker-app-india). Money View similarly pivoted into loan distribution (https://www.mblpfin.com/writing/best-expense-tracker-apps-india-2026). Meanwhile Google tightened the SMS/call-log permission regime for financial apps (noted with dates in https://themoneydecoded.com/blog/best-expense-tracking-apps), weakening SMS-based auto-capture. And the spending that a bank-sync app structurally misses — cash — is where Indian low-value life happens: RBI's retail payment habits survey found **cash remains the most preferred mode for regular expenses, preferred for transactions up to ₹500** (https://rbidocs.rbi.org.in/rdocs/Bulletin/PDFs/04AR_26042021C7C9371E047E4AFCACA834ECCBBB1152.PDF); even after the UPI wave, an RBI economist's study put **cash at ~60% of consumer expenditure as of March 2024** (https://www.business-standard.com/finance/news/digital-payments-double-in-3-years-cash-transactions-fall-rbi-report-124102300571_1.html), and the A.T. Kearney × Amazon Pay "How Urban India Pays" survey found cash still preferred for offline purchases with 90% of online payments digital (https://www.fortuneindia.com/macro/90-go-digital-for-online-payments-cash-still-preferred-for-offline-purchases-report/117503). Daily tiffin/dabbawala-style recurring meal spends sit exactly in this cash + low-value + informal-merchant zone; **no source directly quantifies tiffin-service tracking failure — that link is our product hypothesis, not pinned evidence**.

**Meanwhile the transaction stream PFOS must summarize is enormous and micro-ticketed.** UPI hit a record **21.63 billion transactions in Dec 2025** worth ₹27.97 lakh crore, ~698 million/day (https://www.thehindubusinessline.com/economy/upi-volumes-rise-to-record-2163-billion-in-dec-around-300-lakh-crore-of-upi-transactions-made-in-cy2025/article70460654.ece; corroborated https://www.cnbcv18.com/.../upi-volumes-value-touch-all-time-highs-december-data-2025-ws-l-19811192.htm and https://www.business-standard.com/industry/news/upi-ends-2025-strong-note-record-transactions-december-126010100687_1.html); CY2025 totaled ~228 billion transactions (~₹300 lakh crore) vs 172 billion in 2024 (same BusinessLine piece). UPI's average ticket fell from ₹3,872 (FY17) to ₹1,525 (FY24) as it absorbed small-value purchases, and UPI's P2M volume share hit 87% by FY24 (RBI study, https://www.business-standard.com/finance/news/digital-payments-double-in-3-years-cash-transactions-fall-rbi-report-124102300571_1.html). **Implication: daily transaction counts per person are rising sharply, so manual logging is being asked to scale against more transactions per day while India's aggregator-tracker market has hollowed out.**

## 2. What keeps capture alive past the ~3-week death zone

The convergence across HCI research, government usability testing, and modern app design yields four repeatable conditions — plus one framing principle:

- **Capture from where users already are — chat/voice, not forms.** The single clearest lever: reduce entry from a multi-step app-flow to one spoken/typed sentence. The BLS study found forgetting-to-open-the-app was the killer and participants favored voice input (https://www.bls.gov/cex/research_papers/pdf/evaluation-of-financial-application-software-report.pdf). Indian vendors now compete explicitly on voice-second claims (a voice tracker logging "a ₹350 grocery run in 4 seconds" — vendor claim, unverified: https://www.mrbottomline.club/writing/best-expense-tracker-apps-india-2026), and chat-based capture (WhatsApp) is positioned as "tracking adapts to behavior you already have" (vendor: https://www.gaussapp.com/en/blog/why-financial-apps-fail.html). A separate survey found **weekly check-ins outperform daily real-time logging** — except among voice loggers, whose friction is low enough to sustain daily logging (vendor aggregation, unverified: https://aimoneyvault.app/resources/articles/expense-tracking-statistics-2026).

- **Log at the point of purchase, not from memory.** Reconstructing spend at end-of-day is the documented failure mode: "you log expenses at the end of the day... you've forgotten which coffee was Tuesday's" (vendor essay summarizing the pattern: https://www.vocash.app/blog/why-manual-expense-tracking-fails), corroborated by BLS participants who "kept forgetting." Automatic capture (SMS parsing, notification capture, agent-generated entries) removes memory from the loop entirely — the property that made Walnut beloved before its pivot (https://spendctrl.com/guides/axio-walnut-alternative).

- **Make the tool self-monitoring, judgment-free — "awareness, not restriction."** Epstein et al. show users quit because the app made them "feel guilty every time I tracked an expense that was not a necessity" (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5428074). YNAB's philosophy is explicitly non-punitive: spending is not bad; budgets change "without guilt, stress, or second-guessing" when life changes ("Roll With The Punches": https://www.ynab.com/blog/spending-money-is-not-bad); its brand term is "spendfulness — knowing yourself and spending accordingly... no guilt, no second-guessing, no mental math" (https://www.ynab.com/guide/foundations-the-ynab-method). Monarch's default is **Flex Budgeting** — "a simple focus on just one number" (flexible spending), built "based on the reality that some expenses vary each month" (https://www.monarch.com/features/budgeting, https://help.monarchmoney.com/hc/en-us/articles/360048883631-Creating-Your-Budget-in-Monarch). Copilot is positioned as offering "awareness rather than restriction" and a "gentle approach" for users who are already decent with money (community/comparison review: https://beancount.io/forum/t/copilot-vs-monarch-vs-ynab-which-premium-budget-app-is-worth-it/98; comparison: https://www.dollarscout.net/personal-finance/compare/monarch-vs-copilot).

- **Coping planning + designed-for-lapse, not all-or-nothing.** Long-term tracker users survive via **coping planning** — pre-decided strategies for anticipated lapses (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33464216) — and the personal-informatics literature explicitly asks designers to treat lapses as a design moment ("Reconsidering the Device in the Drawer": https://depstein.net/assets/pubs/depstein_ubi16.pdf), e.g., altered weekly reports computed from a short history. Habit-tracking-app research finds **reminders function as behavioral cues that reduce reliance on memory during early habit formation** (https://doi.org/10.1007/s41347-026-00707-2). For PFOS: the agent can *fill the missed entries from memory of the conversation*, so a missed day degrades gracefully instead of triggering the guilt-abandonment spiral.

- **Visible payoff early.** Tracking alone doesn't change behavior; awareness of patterns does, and an incomplete record feels worthless — which is itself demotivating (vendor synthesis, directionally consistent with the Epstein papers: https://spendtrak.app/blog/why-expense-tracking-fails). BudgeFlow-style statistical pages note the same retention logic — data that feels "overwhelming rather than actionable" drives quits (vendor/unverified: https://budgeflow.com/statistics). PFOS's natural advantage: the agent turns the log into a narrative report for the human automatically.

## 3. Conflicting / cautionary findings

- **"85% abandon in weeks" and "70%+ quit in 30 days" appear only in vendor blogs** (Gauss, BudgeFlow) with no traceable methodology — do not cite as hard stats. RevenueCat's category-level D35 download-to-paid (~1.7% in 2024) is the citation-backed proxy for the same pattern.
- **Strictness split:** YNAB users in community discussions report loving the rigid method yet feeling "restricted" when plans change (https://beancount.io/forum/t/copilot-vs-monarch-vs-ynab-which-premium-budget-app-is-worth-it/98; https://www.reddit.com/r/MonarchMoney/comments/1ep0e4o/monarch_vs_ynab_anyone_who_has_used_both). Monarch itself saw a structural churn pattern: its zero-based vs cash-flow positioning is contested among users; there's no peer-reviewed head-to-head proving gentle beats strict — this remains a UX-philosophy disagreement, not a settled result.
- **AI caution:** RevenueCat 2026 shows AI apps churn 30% faster than non-AI (https://www.revenuecat.com/state-of-subscription-apps) — an AI-first app must beat a known category headwind.
- **UPI ≠ full capture:** P2P transfers (37% of volume) don't identify the underlying spend, and ~52–60% of expenditure may still be cash (RBI, https://www.business-standard.com/finance/news/digital-payments-double-in-3-years-cash-transactions-fall-rbi-report-124102300571_1.html) — auto-capture alone won't suffice; NL capture of cash/tiffin spend remains necessary.
